# Employee handbook

## Welcome

Welcome to Nordlys Analytics. This handbook explains how we work together, what you can expect from the company and what the company expects from you. Read it in your first week and keep it at hand afterwards; most questions new colleagues ask in their first months are answered somewhere in these pages. When something here is unclear or out of date, tell the people team, because the handbook is only useful when it matches what we actually do. We are a small company of about sixty people spread over two offices, one in Copenhagen and one in Aarhus, and many of us work from home part of the week. We value plain speaking, finishing what we start and helping each other out when a deadline gets close.

## Our offices

The Copenhagen office is on the third floor of the old printing house near the harbour. The front door opens with your badge from seven in the morning until eight in the evening; outside those hours you need the code from the facilities manager. There are two meeting rooms that can be booked in the shared calendar and a quiet room that cannot be booked at all, so that it stays free for phone calls and concentrated work. The Aarhus office is smaller and shares a kitchen with the architects next door. Please clean up after yourself in both kitchens. Bicycles go in the courtyard racks, not in the stairwell, because the fire inspector has complained twice.

## Working hours

A full-time position is thirty-seven hours a week. We do not clock in or out, and nobody counts your hours as long as the work gets done and you are reachable during the core hours from ten to three on weekdays. If you need to leave early for a doctor or to pick up a child, just put it in your calendar. Overtime should be the exception: when you regularly work more than forty hours, talk to your manager, because it usually means that a project is under-staffed or badly planned, and that is a problem for the company to solve, not for you to absorb in the evenings.

## Working from home

You may work from home up to three days a week after your first month. Agree on the days with your team, so that everyone is in the office together at least one day a week. The company pays for a desk chair and a second screen for your home office, up to a limit of four thousand kroner, once every five years. Make sure your home network is protected with a password, never work on customer data in a café without the company VPN, and lock your screen when you leave the computer, even at home.

## Holidays

Everyone has twenty-five days of paid holiday a year plus five extra days, which we call the sixth holiday week. The holiday year follows the Danish rules: holiday is earned month by month and can be taken from the first of September. Plan your summer holiday before the first of April so that teams can cover for each other, and take at least three weeks of it in one go between May and September. Holiday that has not been taken by the end of the year can be moved to the next year only after agreement with your manager, and at most five days can be moved.

## Sick leave

When you are ill, send a message to your manager before nine in the morning on the first day, and keep them informed if it lasts more than a couple of days. You do not need a doctor's note for short illnesses, but after fourteen days we will ask for a talk about how we can help you get back. If your child is ill, you can take the first and the second day off with pay. Please do not come to the office with a fever or a bad cold; it is better to rest at home and work later than to infect half of the office.

## Computers and phones

You get a laptop on your first day, set up by the IT team with disk encryption, the password manager and the company VPN. Do not install software from unknown sources, and ask the IT team before you connect your own devices to the office network. Lost or stolen equipment must be reported the same day, so that it can be locked remotely. You may use the laptop for private things within reason, but remember that it belongs to the company. Mobile phones are offered to those who are on call or travel often; the subscription includes free calls in the EU.

## Travel

Business trips are booked through the travel agency in the shared mailbox, not privately, so that we have insurance and a record of where people are. Take the train for journeys within Denmark and to Hamburg; fly only when the train takes more than six hours. Economy class is the rule. Hotels should be close to where the meetings are and cost at most one thousand five hundred kroner a night in Scandinavia. Remember your yellow health insurance card and the travel insurance certificate, which you find on the intranet under travel.

## Passwords and security

Use the password manager for every work account and let it make long random passwords. Turn on two-step login wherever it is offered, and never share a password in a chat, a mail or on a sticky note, not even with a colleague who says it is urgent. Phishing mails often look like they come from the bank, from a delivery service or from our own director; when a mail asks you to click a link and log in, or to pay an invoice in a hurry, call the sender on a number you already know before you do anything. If you think you clicked something you should not have, tell the IT team at once. Nobody gets blamed for reporting a mistake early, while a mistake that is hidden can cost the whole company a great deal.

## Customers and confidentiality

Much of our work is with data that customers trust us with: sales figures, patient numbers, energy use of whole towns. Keep it in the project folders that the customer has agreed to, never copy it to a private computer or a USB stick, and delete working copies when a project closes. Do not talk about customers by name in public places, on social media or in talks, unless the customer has said yes in writing. When a customer asks what we do with their data, refer them to the data protection officer, who keeps the records of processing and the agreements with our subcontractors.

## Meetings

Keep meetings short and invite only the people who need to be there. Every meeting has an agenda in the invitation and somebody who writes down what was decided, who does what and by when. Start on time, also when people are missing, and end five minutes before the hour so that people can get to the next one. The Monday meeting at half past nine is for the whole company and takes fifteen minutes: news, who is away and what is due this week. Cameras are on in video meetings when you can, because it is easier to talk to a face than to a name.

## Lunch and the kitchen

The company pays for lunch in both offices four days a week; on Fridays we order something together. Tell the kitchen by Thursday if you are vegetarian, allergic or will not be there next week. Fruit, coffee and tea are free at all times. The dishwasher is emptied by whoever finds it clean, and the fridge is cleared every Friday at three, so take home what you want to keep.

## Phones on call

The operations team has one person on call outside working hours, in weeks that rotate between six colleagues. The person on call carries the company phone, answers alerts from the monitoring system within fifteen minutes and writes a short note in the log about what happened and what was done. A week on call is paid with a fixed supplement, and every call that takes more than half an hour is paid as overtime. Swap weeks with a colleague if you need to, and update the rota in the calendar when you do.

## Expenses and reimbursement

You get your money back for expenses you have paid yourself on behalf of the company, such as taxis, train tickets, meals during travel and small purchases for a project. Take a photo of every receipt with the expense app on the day you get it, write what it was for and which project it belongs to, and submit the month's expenses by the fifth of the following month. Your manager approves them within a week, and the money is paid with your next salary. Meals with customers need the names of the guests on the receipt. Alcohol is reimbursed only at dinners with customers. Expenses older than three months cannot be reimbursed, so do not let the receipts pile up in your wallet.

## Parental leave

Parents have the right to leave under Danish law, and the company pays full salary for up to twenty-four weeks for the parent who gives birth and up to sixteen weeks for the other parent. Tell your manager as early as you feel comfortable, at the latest three months before the leave starts, so that we can plan who takes over your work. You can split the leave into several periods and come back part-time for a while. During the leave you keep your laptop and your access, but nobody expects you to read your mail.

## Training and conferences

Each employee has a training budget of fifteen thousand kroner a year for courses, books and conferences. Agree with your manager on what you want to learn and why it helps the work. When you go to a conference, share what you learned with your team afterwards, for example in a short talk on Friday afternoon. Certifications that the company asks you to take are paid in full and the exam days count as working days.

## Salary and pension

Salary is paid on the last working day of the month. The company pays eight percent of your salary into a pension scheme, and you pay four percent yourself; you can choose to pay more. Salaries are reviewed once a year in March, based on a talk with your manager about the past year. Health insurance through the pension company covers treatment by a physiotherapist and a psychologist.

## Leaving the company

If you want to leave, tell your manager first, then send your resignation in writing. The notice period is one month for you during the first six months and then follows your contract. On your last day, hand in your laptop, phone, badge and keys to the facilities manager, and make sure that your files are stored in the shared drives and not only on your own laptop. We hold a short exit talk to learn what we could do better.

## Health and safety

Every office has a first-aid kit in the kitchen and a defibrillator by the front door; the instructions are printed on the lid. Two colleagues in each office are trained first-aiders, and their names are on the notice board. When the fire alarm sounds, leave by the nearest stairs, not the lift, and meet in the square in front of the building, where the facilities manager counts who is out. Tell the safety representative about loose cables, broken chairs and anything else that could hurt somebody. Once a year we look at the working environment together, and you can ask for a desk that can be raised and lowered if you have trouble with your back or neck.
